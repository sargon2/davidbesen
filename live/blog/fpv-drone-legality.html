The Legality of FPV Drone Piloting

David Besen, 08/19/2026. I am not a lawyer.

Several of my friends are into FPV drone piloting, and I'm interested in taking up the hobby as well. But, what I'm discovering is not a fun, skill-based hobby - it's a rabbit hole of poorly written, draconian laws. There are many cumbersome FAA and FCC laws around the hobby, and I'm going to dive into the particular laws that I think are the biggest reasons to avoid the hobby. (This applies only to the US.) A lot of people argue "You speed on the road, so why not disobey these laws too?" That's technically true, you can do that and risk fines, but it doesn't leave me with a fun, happy feeling inside. The main reason to fly FPV drones is for fun, and at least for me, it's not fun to break the law. Being forced to break even an unenforced law is a valid reason to stay away from a hobby.

1. The FAA Spotter Requirement

Drone activity per the FAA is strictly divided into recreational and non-recreational. For recreational flight, the applicable law is 49 U.S.C §44809, "Exception for limited recreational operations of unmanned aircraft":

(a) In General.-Except as provided in subsection (e), and notwithstanding chapter 447 of title 49, United States Code, a person may operate a small unmanned aircraft without specific certification or operating authority from the Federal Aviation Administration if the operation adheres to all of the following limitations:

(1) The aircraft is flown strictly for recreational purposes.

(2) The aircraft is operated in accordance with or within the programming of a community-based organization's set of safety guidelines that are developed in coordination with the Federal Aviation Administration.

(3) The aircraft is flown within the visual line of sight of the person operating the aircraft or a visual observer co-located and in direct communication with the operator.

(4) The aircraft is operated in a manner that does not interfere with and gives way to any manned aircraft.

(5) In Class B, Class C, or Class D airspace or within the lateral boundaries of the surface area of Class E airspace designated for an airport, the operator obtains prior authorization from the Administrator or designee before operating and complies with all airspace restrictions and prohibitions.

(6) Except for circumstances when the Administrator establishes alternative altitude ceilings or as otherwise authorized in section (c), in Class G airspace, the aircraft is flown from the surface to not more than 400 feet above ground level and complies with all airspace and flight restrictions and prohibitions established under this subtitle, such as special use airspace designations and temporary flight restrictions.

(7) The operator has passed an aeronautical knowledge and safety test described in subsection (g) and maintains proof of test passage to be made available to the Administrator or law enforcement upon request.

(8) The aircraft is registered and marked in accordance with chapter 441 of this title and proof of registration is made available to the Administrator or a designee of the Administrator or law enforcement upon request.

49 U.S.C §44809

The relevant part is (3). "co-located and in direct communication" means the visual observer must be standing next to the pilot, so the audience at an FPV race doesn't count. Presumably even a person standing next to the pilot doesn't count, unless they have explicitly accepted the responsibility of being the visual observer.

For most non-recreational flight, the pilot must obtain a Remote Pilot Certificate. In addition, the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) § 107.31 says this:

(a) With vision that is unaided by any device other than corrective lenses, the remote pilot in command, the visual observer (if one is used), and the person manipulating the flight control of the small unmanned aircraft system must be able to see the unmanned aircraft throughout the entire flight in order to:

(1) Know the unmanned aircraft's location;

(2) Determine the unmanned aircraft's attitude, altitude, and direction of flight;

(3) Observe the airspace for other air traffic or hazards; and

(4) Determine that the unmanned aircraft does not endanger the life or property of another.

(b) Throughout the entire flight of the small unmanned aircraft, the ability described in paragraph (a) of this section must be exercised by either:

(1) The remote pilot in command and the person manipulating the flight controls of the small unmanned aircraft system; or

(2) A visual observer.

14 CFR § 107.31

This is very clear that FPV goggles are not good enough to fly, according to the law. The visual observer requirement is actually a compromise - before the visual observer text was added to the law, it was illegal to fly using FPV at all, because the pilot was required to maintain unaided sight. Only later did they add the ability to use a visual observer.

This requirement is very cumbersome. Zero of my friends use a visual observer when they go fly. There are not formal visual observers at freestyle events, bandos, or outdoor races (indoor areas aren't governed by the FAA). FPV celebrity Joshua Bardwell does not use visual observers when flying in many of his videos. When I searched for recorded FPV flights on Youtube, 10 of the top 10 results were not using a visual observer. So, this law is not having its intended effect. All it's doing is making the most common type of FPV flight illegal, rather than improving its safety. It is not improving the safety of real aircraft pilots because drone pilots are neither obeying the law nor ceasing their activities. This law is ineffective and overbearing.

Note also that the text of the law is a bit self-contradictory. Part (a) uses the word "and" to say the remote pilot, the visual observer, and the person manipulating the controls must all three have "vision that is unaided", meaning using FPV goggles at all is illegal. But part (b) uses the terms "either" and "or", implying the pilot doesn't need "vision that is unaided" provided they have an observer. The FAA has clarified that the intention is "or", but haven't updated the text to remove the apparent contradiction.

2. The FCC Identification Requirement

For radio communications to be legal, they must be authorized by the FCC. The FCC authorizes devices certified under "Part 15", and also allows "amateur" radio communications via part 97 of the CFR, among other authorization paths.

Some FPV drone equipment is certified as part 15; for example, Radiomaster radios are part 15, meaning they're allowed to be used without any license. But, if a device isn't certified under part 15, it has to be otherwise authorized. One way to get something authorized is to use the "amateur" frequencies - these are frequencies intended for amateur ham radio operators, and all drone VTX transmitters that I'm aware of use these amateur frequencies. To be allowed to be used, the operator has to have a ham technician or better license, and the transmitter has to comply with part 97. Part 97 (actually the definitions preceeding it, in § 97.3 (a)) say to use amateur frequencies, you have to be "interested in radio technique solely with a personal aim and without pecuniary interest." This means it's illegal to use any FPV equipment that uses these amateur bands for any kind of profit, which might include posting FPV videos to monetization-enabled Youtube channels.

One of the requirements of using amateur frequencies is you must identify yourself. § 97.119 parts a and b are:

(a) Each amateur station, except a space station or telecommand station, must transmit its assigned call sign on its transmitting channel at the end of each communication, and at least every 10 minutes during a communication, for the purpose of clearly making the source of the transmissions from the station known to those receiving the transmissions. No station may transmit unidentified communications or signals, or transmit as the station call sign, any call sign not authorized to the station.

(b) The call sign must be transmitted with an emission authorized for the transmitting channel in one of the following ways:

(1) By a CW emission. When keyed by an automatic device used only for identification, the speed must not exceed 20 words per minute;

(2) By a phone emission in the English language. Use of a phonetic alphabet as an aid for correct station identification is encouraged;

(3) By a RTTY emission using a specified digital code when all or part of the communications are transmitted by a RTTY or data emission;

(4) By an image emission conforming to the applicable transmission standards, either color or monochrome, of § 73.682(a) of the FCC Rules when all or part of the communications are transmitted in the same image emission.

47 CFR § 97.119(a)–(b)

"Telecommand Station" is defined as "An amateur station that transmits communications to initiate, modify or terminate functions of a space station." Drone VTXes are transmitting video. No VTX I'm aware of has the capability to identify any way other than video. The video text references § 73.682(a). § 73.682(a) is:

(a)-(c) [Reserved]

47 CFR § 73.682(a)-(c)

This means that the CFR is claiming you must comply with nonexistant transmission standards. Is that possible for any human to do? I don't know.

Some drone operators interpret this to mean you have to put your ham callsign in your drone's video OSD. There is nothing in the CFR that says it's legal to do that. But surely it complies with the spirit of the law, if not the letter. Right?

The identification requirement applies to other transmissions as well, not just the video signal. There's an exception for the control link that was put in place for AMA model aircraft. But, there is also telemetry to worry about. The receiver in the drone transmits telemetry back to the controller, using amateur bands. Some receivers (e.g. some Radiomaster receivers) have part 15, so don't need to identify. But many, including every receiver made by BetaFPV as far as I can tell, don't. Part 97 specifically calls out telemetry in § 97.217, saying it's "not considered to be codes or ciphers", but does not exempt it from the identification requirement. That means your receiver, when sending telemetry, has to broadcast your ham callsign using an approved format such as RTTY. No receiver I'm aware of has this feature, meaning all non-part-15 drone receivers I'm aware of are illegal to operate.

3. Illegal Per the FCC Even if Used Correctly

Drone VTXes are commonly manufactured in other countries such as China. Those other countries have different laws than the US does, so it makes sense to design the transmitter to be able to transmit on various different frequencies. Unfortunately, the FCC says that's illegal.

FCC Enforcement Advisory DA-18-581 says:

If an AV transmitter is capable of operating outside of the amateur frequency bands, it cannot be advertised, sold, or operated within the United States without an FCC equipment certification.

Enforcement Bureau Issues Advisory on Drone Transmitter Equipment

This means that if your transmitter can broadcast outside the amateur band, even if you don't use your transmitter outside the amateur band, it's illegal to operate at all.

An enforcement advisory is not a law. The laws in this area are quite complex, and the advisory is the FCC's interpretation of the law. I was able to find several related laws, such as 47 CFR § 2.805(a) that says equipment which requires authorization can't be operated without it, and 47 U.S.C. § 302a(b) that says no person may "use devices" that fail to comply with FCC regulations. The FCC clearly intends to treat these devices as illegal, and has enforced the law that way in the past - for example, they fined HobbyKing almost $3 million in 2020 specifically for selling such devices.

Devices which can operate outside of the amateur bands very likely won't be certified. If you can find a certified device, that means it's legal to use with the appropriate license, provided it's used in a legal manner. You can tell if your device is certified by whether or not it has a valid FCC ID on the packaging, in the manual, or on the device itself. Some drone VTXes are certified, but they're few and far between. No tiny whoop-sized drone VTX that I've been able to find is explicitly certified, and I suspect if one out there is certified, it will be severely crippled in range or capability.
